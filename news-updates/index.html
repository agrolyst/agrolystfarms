Markets·Explainer

Why the agrifood value chain is about more than growing food

Food moves through connected stages, from inputs and production to handling, transport, processing, retail and consumption. Gaps between stages can affect quality, costs and the price received by producers.

Read the full perspective

Looking at the whole chain helps identify where value is lost or could be improved. Farmers may benefit from understanding buyer specifications and harvest timing; traders and processors may need more consistent volumes and handling; buyers need dependable quality and delivery. No single fix suits every crop or location, so practical decisions should start with local evidence, relationships and the costs each participant faces.

Farm business·Practical guide

Small farm records that can improve everyday decisions

Records do not have to begin with complex software. A consistent note of inputs, labour, harvests, sales and payments can help make farm decisions less dependent on memory.

Read the full perspective

Start with a few fields that match the operation: date, activity, quantity, cost, person responsible and result. Record purchases and sales promptly, separate household and business transactions where possible, and review the information at regular intervals. Keep receipts where available and protect personal financial information. Records are most useful when they inform a decision, such as adjusting an input plan or comparing crop cycles, rather than simply accumulating in a notebook.

Markets·Market insight

Building stronger links between producers and buyers

Market access involves more than finding a buyer once. Clear expectations around quality, quantity, timing, price and payment help build relationships that can last.

Read the full perspective

Before agreeing a sale, clarify the grade or condition expected, how quantities will be measured, who handles transport, when payment is due and what happens if delivery changes. Where possible, compare more than one offer and calculate the costs of meeting each buyer's requirements. Written records of agreed terms reduce confusion. Producers and buyers should verify counterparties and avoid commitments they cannot realistically fulfil.

Food systems·Explainer

Why post-harvest handling deserves attention

Careful handling, appropriate storage and timely movement can help protect food quality after harvest, though the right approach depends on the crop and local conditions.

Read the full perspective

Damage can occur during harvesting, sorting, packaging, loading and storage. Clean containers, shade where appropriate, gentle handling and suitable storage conditions are useful starting points, but perishable crops have different temperature, moisture and ventilation needs. Seek crop-specific guidance before investing in equipment. Tracking where damage occurs can help identify whether the biggest improvement lies in harvest practice, packaging, transport or storage.

Farm business·Planning

Practical ways to think about farm risk and resilience

Weather, pests, input costs, market shifts and cash-flow pressure can all affect farm plans. Identifying risks early helps make contingency planning more deliberate.

Read the full perspective

List the risks that are most relevant to the specific farm, estimate how likely and disruptive each could be, and identify actions that are realistic with available resources. Depending on the context, these might include maintaining basic records, checking trusted local forecasts, planning input purchases, protecting soil and water, diversifying carefully or discussing supply terms in advance. Resilience is not a guarantee against loss; it is the practice of preparing for uncertainty and learning from outcomes.

Food systems·Food culture

Food culture connects farms, kitchens and communities

Food traditions carry knowledge about ingredients, preparation, seasonal availability and the role of meals in community life.

Read the full perspective

Local food knowledge can help preserve recipes, encourage appreciation for regional crops and connect consumers with the people who grow and process ingredients. It also evolves as communities adapt to new tools, tastes and circumstances. Recording food stories respectfully means recognising their sources, asking permission before sharing personal accounts and avoiding the assumption that one tradition represents everyone in a region.

Technology·Digital tools

Choosing digital tools that solve a real farm problem

Digital tools are most useful when they address a clear need and fit the user's connectivity, skills, budget and working routine.

Read the full perspective

Begin with the task rather than the app: do you need to track inventory, record sales, coordinate workers or communicate with buyers? Compare the cost, offline access, data export options, support and privacy implications. Test a simple workflow before moving important records. A paper register or spreadsheet can be appropriate in some situations; digital adoption is not automatically an improvement unless it makes the work more reliable or decisions more informed.

Farm business·Readiness

What to prepare before seeking agribusiness finance

A clear business plan, realistic cost assumptions and a transparent use for funds can help a business explain its needs to a potential finance partner.

Read the full perspective

Prepare a concise description of the business, customers, operating model, funding purpose, budget and expected cash flow. Separate assumptions from confirmed orders or revenue, explain key risks and gather available records. Compare the total cost and conditions of any offer, including repayment schedules, collateral, fees and consequences of default. No funding outcome is guaranteed; verify organisations and written terms carefully, and never pay an unverified person who promises certain approval.

Markets·Collaboration

When collective action can help small producers

Working together may help producers coordinate volumes, share selected costs or exchange information, but cooperation needs clear rules and trust.

Read the full perspective

Groups should agree on responsibilities, decision-making, record keeping, quality standards, how expenses are shared and how members can leave. Start with a specific shared need and test the arrangement on a manageable scale. Collective action is not always the best option for every activity, and it cannot remove market risk. Transparent governance and regular communication help members assess whether the benefits justify the effort.

Learning opportunities can combine classroom knowledge with communication, record keeping, teamwork and an understanding of real business processes.

Food systems·Quality

Quality and safety are part of building food trust

Good food handling practices, cleanliness and clear responsibilities are important at each stage of food preparation and distribution.

Read the full perspective

Food safety depends on the product, process and applicable requirements. Businesses should follow current local regulations and authoritative, product-specific guidance rather than relying on general advice alone. Keep relevant cleaning and handling procedures clear, train people on their responsibilities and respond appropriately to complaints or suspected contamination. A strong quality culture is built through consistent practice, traceable decisions and a willingness to correct problems.
